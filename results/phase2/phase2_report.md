## 2. Findings

* Error falls as CNOT^-1.01 for the first-order formula and CNOT^-2.07 for the second-order formula (mean over n = [4, 5, 6]). Gaining one decade of accuracy costs about 9.9x more CNOTs at first order versus 3.04x at second order.
* At equal r, second order is more accurate by a factor of 1.03 to 36430.2 (median 113.3) while using only 2(n-1) extra CNOTs and 3(n-1) extra gates - a fixed cost that does not grow with r.
* At a matched CNOT budget, second order reaches a lower error in 102 of 105 measured budgets, by 32.3% to more than 99.99% (median 99.1%). It is worse at 3 budget(s), all at coarse steps (r <= 1), where its fixed 2(n-1)-CNOT overhead buys an extra step for the first-order formula instead of accuracy.
* At a matched total gate budget, second order reaches a lower error in 102 of 105 measured budgets, by 39.9% to more than 99.99% (median 99.1%). It is worse at 3 budget(s), all at coarse steps (r <= 1), where its fixed 2(n-1)-CNOT overhead buys an extra step for the first-order formula instead of accuracy.
* For a fixed achieved accuracy where both formulas succeed, second order needs 70.7% to 99.5% fewer gates (68.8% to 99.5% fewer CNOTs).
* Measured resources match the closed forms exactly (max |difference| = 0 over 216 rows): CNOT is exactly linear in (n-1) and in r, so the resource cost of a chain is set by its bonds, not its qubits.
* Logical depth grows linearly with r but only by 0.43% across n = 4...6 at fixed r, because the conservative scheduler serialises bonds; a hardware-aware schedule would change this, so no depth-based claim beyond 'conservative logical estimate' is made.
* No measured point reaches the 1e-10 numerical floor (smallest measured error 1.26e-09), so every error reported here is truncation error rather than round-off.
* Answer to the phase question: second order is the better use of every resource measured (steps, CNOTs, total gates), and its advantage is largest when the accuracy target is tight; the extra cost is a constant 2(n-1) CNOTs, not a per-step penalty.

A slope of about -1 means error falls like `1/CNOT`; -2 means `1/CNOT^2`, i.e. the
second-order formula converts entangling gates into accuracy quadratically faster.

## 6. Scaling with qubit count and step count

* Analytical formulas reproduced by every measured row: max |difference| = 0 over 216 rows (all match: True).
* Order 1: CNOT vs (n-1) slope +1.00 (theory +1.00); total gates vs r slope +1.00 (theory +1.00); depth vs r slope +0.98; depth vs (n-1) slope +0.01.
* Order 2: CNOT vs (n-1) slope +1.00 (theory +1.00); total gates vs r slope +0.98 (theory +1.00); depth vs r slope +0.96; depth vs (n-1) slope +0.01.
* Order 1: at fixed r=200 the depth varies by only 0.43% between the smallest and largest chain ({4: 1403, 5: 1406, 6: 1409}).
* Order 2: at fixed r=200 the depth varies by only 0.43% between the smallest and largest chain ({4: 1409, 5: 1412, 6: 1415}).

So gate counts scale linearly in `(n-1)` and linearly in `r`; depth scales linearly in
`r` but is almost independent of `n`, because the chain is simulated sequentially bond by
bond and no bond parallelism is exploited by the conservative scheduler.

## 9. Limits of these conclusions

* Gate counts are logical and assume all-to-all connectivity; no compilation, native gate
  set or error-correction overhead is modelled.
* Depth is one conservative ASAP schedule (no bond colouring, no hardware timing);
  differences of a few percent in depth are not meaningful.
* Only `n = 4, 5, 6` and `t = 1, J = h = 1` were measured; nothing here is extrapolated
  beyond that range, and no claim is made about larger systems.
* Errors near the floor are round-off, not physical accuracy.
* No noise model: these are ideal-simulation costs, not device predictions.
